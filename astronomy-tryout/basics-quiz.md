# Astronomy basics check — 20 questions

Last year was a year ago. This is a **foundations** sweep: the stuff a Division C test
assumes you already know before it asks you anything about galaxies. Answer out loud
or on paper — the key is at the bottom, with what to re-study for anything you miss.

No calculators needed except for Q12 and Q18.

---

1. Put these spectral classes in order from **hottest to coolest**: G, M, O, K, A, B, F.

2. Star A has apparent magnitude +2, star B has +7. Which looks brighter from Earth, and by what factor?

3. What is a parsec, and how do you get distance from parallax?

4. On an H-R diagram, what are the two axes, and where do **most** stars sit?

5. Where is the Sun on the main sequence, and roughly how long will it stay there?

6. A star is 10× more massive than the Sun. Is it roughly 10×, 100×, or 10,000× more luminous?

7. A spectral line normally at 656 nm appears at 660 nm. Is the source moving toward or away from us, and how would you compute the speed?

8. State Kepler's third law and the shortcut version used for binaries.

9. What are Kirchhoff's three kinds of spectra, and what produces each?

10. A star's temperature doubles. By what factor does its luminosity per unit surface area change? What happens to the wavelength of its peak emission?

11. What is the difference between apparent magnitude, absolute magnitude, and luminosity?

12. A Cepheid has a period of 10 days. Using M_V = −2.76 log₁₀(P) − 1.40, what is its absolute magnitude?

13. Why is a Cepheid useful for measuring distance but a typical red giant is not?

14. What does Hubble's law say, and what is the currently accepted value of H₀?

15. Order these distance methods from nearest to farthest reach: Cepheids, parallax, Type Ia supernovae, Tully–Fisher, Hubble's law.

16. In the Hubble sequence, what changes as you go from Sa → Sc?

17. What is the observational evidence that spiral galaxies contain dark matter?

18. A star has a parallax of 0.01 arcsec and an apparent magnitude of 8. What is its absolute magnitude?

19. What are the three possible endpoints of a star's life, and what mass ranges lead to each?

20. What is the difference between a meteoroid, an asteroid, and a comet?

# Answer key

**1.** **O B A F G K M** (hottest → coolest). Mnemonic: *Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me.* O ≈ 30,000+ K, M ≈ 3,000 K.

**2.** **Star A** (+2 is brighter; smaller/negative magnitude = brighter). Difference of 5 magnitudes = factor of exactly 100, so **A is 100× brighter**.

**3.** A parsec is the distance at which 1 AU subtends 1 arcsecond — 3.26 ly, 206,265 AU. And **d (pc) = 1 / p (arcsec)**. Watch for parallax quoted in milliarcsec: divide by 1000 first.

**4.** X-axis = **temperature** (or spectral class/color), plotted **backwards** (hot on the left); Y-axis = **luminosity** (or absolute magnitude). Most stars sit on the **main sequence**, a diagonal band from hot/luminous upper-left to cool/faint lower-right. Giants sit upper-right, white dwarfs lower-left.

**5.** A G2V yellow dwarf, about halfway along: T ≈ 5,800 K, L ≈ 1 L☉. Main-sequence lifetime ≈ 10 billion years total; it's about 4.6 Gyr in, so ~5 Gyr left.

**6.** **~10,000×.** On the main sequence roughly **L ∝ M^3.5**. Mass is the single most important property of a star.

**7.** **Away** — the line is shifted to longer wavelength (redshift). z = Δλ/λ₀ = 4/656 ≈ 0.0061, then **v ≈ cz ≈ 1,830 km/s**. (Only valid for z ≪ 1.)

**8.** The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis: **P² ∝ a³**. For binaries using AU, years, and solar masses: **M_total = a³ / P²**.

**9.** (1) **Continuous** — a hot dense solid, liquid, or high-pressure gas (a blackbody). (2) **Emission (bright-line)** — a hot, thin gas. (3) **Absorption (dark-line)** — continuous source seen through a cooler, thin gas. Stars give absorption spectra: the cool outer layers absorb specific wavelengths.

**10.** Luminosity per area goes as **T⁴**, so **16×**. Peak wavelength goes as 1/T (**Wien's law**), so it **halves** — the peak moves toward the blue/UV.

**11.** **Apparent magnitude m** = how bright it *looks* from Earth (depends on distance). **Absolute magnitude M** = how bright it *would* look at 10 pc — a fair comparison of true output. **Luminosity** = total energy emitted per second (watts), intrinsic, not tied to any magnitude scale.

**12.** **−4.16.** log₁₀(10) = 1 → −2.76(1) − 1.40 = **−4.16**.

**13.** A Cepheid's **period tells you its luminosity** (the period–luminosity relation), so comparing that true luminosity to how faint it looks gives the distance. An ordinary red giant has no such calibrator — you can't know its luminosity just by looking at it.

**14.** Recession velocity is proportional to distance: **v = H₀ d**. Galaxies farther away recede faster — evidence the universe expands. **H₀ ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc** (use 70 unless the test states otherwise).

**15.** **Parallax** (nearest, ~kpc) → **Cepheids** (~20–40 Mpc with HST) → **Tully–Fisher** (~100 Mpc) → **Type Ia supernovae** (Gpc) → **Hubble's law** (farthest / any redshift). Each rung is calibrated by the one below it.

**16.** The **bulge gets smaller**, the **arms get more open**, the **gas fraction and star-formation rate go up**, and the **color gets bluer**. Morphology tracks physics, not just appearance.

**17.** **Rotation curves stay flat** far beyond the visible disk instead of falling off as v ∝ 1/√r (Keplerian). Flat means M(<r) = v²r/G keeps growing while the light doesn't → an extended dark halo. (Also: cluster velocity dispersions, gravitational lensing, the CMB.)

**18.** **M = +3.** d = 1/0.01 = 100 pc. m − M = 5 log₁₀(d/10) = 5 log₁₀(10) = 5, so M = 8 − 5 = **+3**.

**19.** **White dwarf** — low/intermediate mass (≲8 M☉), supported by electron degeneracy, capped at 1.4 M☉ (Chandrasekhar). **Neutron star** — ~8–25 M☉ core. **Black hole** — the most massive (≳25 M☉ core).

**20.** **Asteroid** — rocky/metallic body orbiting the Sun, mostly in the main belt (too small to be a planet, not icy). **Comet** — icy body that grows a coma and tail when it heats up near the Sun. **Meteoroid** — a small rock in space; it becomes a *meteor* (streak) in the atmosphere and a *meteorite* if it lands.
